## 2. Scientific Contribution

The paper develops an engineering framework for interpreting desulfurization performance through the coupled effects of:

- intrinsic reaction kinetics;
- catalytic and adsorptive surface phenomena;
- external interphase mass transfer;
- internal diffusion in porous catalysts and adsorbents;
- sulfur-species-dependent reactivity;
- hydrogen or oxidant availability;
- reactor hydrodynamics;
- downstream separation;
- catalyst or adsorbent durability;
- energy and process-intensification penalties.

The central proposition is that measured sulfur conversion or removal cannot be interpreted reliably from reaction chemistry alone.

Observed performance may be controlled by one mechanism, by several interacting resistances, or by a limitation that changes with:

- conversion;
- operating severity;
- catalyst age;
- particle size;
- feed composition;
- reactor scale;
- treatment time;
- downstream process configuration.

The framework therefore connects reaction engineering, transport analysis, separation performance, and scale-up validation within one diagnostic structure.

## 3. Application Scope

The paper addresses desulfurization of:

- natural gas and acid-gas streams;
- light hydrocarbon streams;
- diesel and middle-distillate fuels;
- heavy petroleum fractions.

Representative process routes include:

- hydrodesulfurization;
- oxidative desulfurization;
- catalytic oxidation and sweetening;
- adsorptive desulfurization;
- reactive adsorptive desulfurization;
- radical-assisted oxidation;
- hybrid reaction–separation systems;
- hydrodynamic process intensification.

The paper is not an exhaustive review or a claim that one technology is universally preferable.

The intended purpose is to identify which physical or chemical limitation must be addressed for a defined feed, reactor, catalyst, adsorbent, and process boundary.

## 4. Reaction–Transport–Separation Framework

The paper considers several controlling or interacting regimes.

### 4.1 Reaction-Controlled Behavior

Intrinsic chemical transformation is slow relative to the relevant transport processes.

Possible engineering responses include:

- improving catalyst activity;
- increasing reactive-species availability;
- changing temperature or pressure;
- improving oxidant activation;
- modifying reaction chemistry.

A reaction-controlled interpretation requires evidence that external and internal transport effects are sufficiently small.

### 4.2 External Mass-Transfer Limitation

Transport from a bulk phase to an interface, catalyst surface, adsorbent, bubble, droplet, or reactive phase is slow relative to reaction.

Relevant variables may include:

- mass-transfer coefficient;
- interfacial area;
- droplet or bubble size;
- mixing;
- phase ratio;
- viscosity;
- flow regime;
- reactor geometry.

An apparent rate increase caused by improved contacting must not automatically be interpreted as intrinsic kinetic enhancement.

### 4.3 Internal Diffusion Limitation

Reaction or adsorption within a porous solid is sufficiently rapid that pore transport reduces internal catalyst or adsorbent utilization.

Relevant diagnostic concepts include:

- Thiele modulus;
- effectiveness factor;
- effective diffusivity;
- particle size;
- pore structure;
- molecular accessibility;
- Weisz–Prater-type screening.

The interpretation depends on the kinetic basis, particle geometry, diffusivity model, and external surface concentration.

### 4.4 Adsorption and Capacity Limitation

Adsorptive desulfurization may be limited by:

- equilibrium capacity;
- competitive adsorption;
- adsorption kinetics;
- external film transfer;
- pore diffusion;
- mass-transfer-zone propagation;
- regeneration;
- irreversible fouling.

High equilibrium capacity alone is not sufficient evidence of practical fixed-bed performance.

### 4.5 Reactant-Utilization Limitation

Hydrogen, oxidant, oxygen, promoter, or another reactive species may be supplied but used inefficiently because of:

- poor distribution;
- interphase transfer resistance;
- decomposition;
- local depletion;
- non-selective side reactions;
- inadequate recycle.

Reactant dosage and useful reactant utilization must therefore be distinguished.

### 4.6 Hydrodynamic Limitation

Nominal residence time does not guarantee equivalent treatment of every fluid element.

Performance may be affected by:

- bypassing;
- channeling;
- stagnant zones;
- incomplete suspension;
- poor wetting;
- phase maldistribution;
- broad residence-time distribution;
- nonuniform pressure or shear.

### 4.7 Separation Limitation

Chemical transformation does not necessarily produce final sulfur removal.

This distinction is particularly important in oxidative desulfurization, where sulfur compounds may be converted to sulfoxides or sulfones but remain in the product unless subsequently removed.

Relevant downstream operations include:

- solvent extraction;
- adsorption;
- phase separation;
- filtration;
- crystallization;
- membrane treatment;
- polishing.

The final product sulfur concentration must therefore be measured after the complete reaction and separation sequence.

### 4.8 Mixed and Transitional Regimes

Real systems may operate under combined or changing control.

Examples include:

- reaction plus liquid–liquid transfer in oxidative desulfurization;
- reaction plus internal diffusion in hydrodesulfurization;
- adsorption capacity plus pore diffusion;
- reactive absorption plus gas–liquid transfer;
- oxidation plus downstream separation;
- initial reaction control followed by deactivation or reactant depletion.

A single regime label may therefore be insufficient.

## 5. Diagnostic Quantities

The paper uses established reaction and transport concepts as diagnostic tools, including:

- apparent and intrinsic rate coefficients;
- characteristic reaction and transfer times;
- Damköhler-type ratios;
- volumetric mass-transfer coefficients;
- Thiele modulus;
- internal effectiveness factor;
- adsorption-capacity relationships;
- cavitation descriptors;
- energy-normalized performance indicators.

These quantities are meaningful only when their:

- units;
- concentration bases;
- phase-volume bases;
- geometrical assumptions;
- system boundaries;
- reference conditions;

are explicitly defined and mutually compatible.

The paper does not establish universal numerical thresholds for every feed, reactor, or technology.

## 6. Sulfur Conversion Versus Sulfur Removal

A central requirement of the framework is to distinguish among:

1. disappearance of an initial sulfur compound;
2. formation of another sulfur-containing species;
3. transfer of sulfur to another phase;
4. temporary adsorption;
5. sulfur retained on solids or equipment;
6. sulfur removed from the final recovered product.

A decrease in sulfur concentration in one measured phase may result from:

- chemical conversion;
- extraction;
- adsorption;
- precipitation;
- deposition;
- dilution;
- sampling loss;
- analytical bias.

A technically defensible assessment should therefore include sulfur accounting across all relevant phases and deposits.

> **Sulfur conversion is not necessarily equivalent to final sulfur removal.**

---

## 7. Hydrodynamic Cavitation and Process Intensification

The paper treats hydrodynamic cavitation as a possible **reaction–transport intensification layer**, not as a universally effective stand-alone desulfurization technology.

Potentially relevant effects may include:

- improved mixing;
- increased interfacial renewal;
- improved dispersion;
- enhanced transfer;
- improved oxidant distribution;
- changes in apparent reaction rate.

These effects must be evaluated against:

- electrical energy demand;
- hydraulic pressure drop;
- oxidant consumption;
- erosion;
- corrosion;
- fouling;
- emulsification;
- product loss;
- downstream separation burden;
- maintenance;
- reliability;
- scale-up uncertainty.

An apparent rate enhancement does not by itself prove:

- intrinsic kinetic enhancement;
- radical-mediated chemistry;
- industrial usefulness;
- scale-up transferability.

A valid assessment requires a defined reference case, a matched thermal control where appropriate, and direct characterization of pressure, flow, temperature, and energy.

## 8. Engineering Purpose

The framework is intended to support technically defensible decisions in:

- experimental design;
- process diagnosis;
- catalyst and adsorbent evaluation;
- reactor selection;
- process-intensification assessment;
- hybrid reaction–separation design;
- validation planning;
- pilot development;
- scale-up analysis.

The central decision principle is:

> **Do not intensify a process before identifying what limits it.**

The preferred intervention is the one that reduces the controlling bottleneck while maintaining acceptable:

- sulfur-removal performance;
- product quality;
- energy demand;
- chemical consumption;
- separation performance;
- material compatibility;
- operability;
- reliability;
- maintainability;
- safety;
- scale-up transferability.

## 9. Relationship to the Executable Repository

The paper provides the principal scientific and engineering framework.

The surrounding repository operationalizes selected parts of that framework through:

- documented equations;
- regime-classification guidance;
- validation guidelines;
- a validated illustrative parameter dataset;
- executable HDS, ODS, and cavitation examples;
- automated scientific and numerical tests;
- continuous integration across supported Python versions.

The repository examples are not direct reproductions of a validated industrial plant.

They are transparent E3 screening implementations intended to demonstrate:

- dimensional consistency;
- limiting behavior;
- resistance accounting;
- sensitivity analysis;
- input validation;
- reproducible calculation.

## 11. Applicability and Limitations

The paper and repository may support:

- preliminary engineering screening;
- identification of controlling mechanisms;
- formulation of testable hypotheses;
- experimental planning;
- sensitivity analysis;
- transparent comparison of process options;
- definition of missing validation evidence.

They do not provide:

- universally valid kinetic constants;
- validated catalyst or adsorbent datasets;
- universal mass-transfer correlations;
- a complete reactor simulator;
- a complete process simulator;
- validated cavitation chemistry;
- proprietary device design information;
- process-safety calculations;
- detailed techno-economic analysis;
- guaranteed product specifications;
- industrial performance warranties.

All conclusions must remain within the feed, operating range, scale, analytical basis, and process boundary actually validated.

## 16. Summary

The paper provides a reaction–transport–separation framework for diagnosing why a desulfurization process performs as observed and which intervention is most technically defensible.

Its principal contribution is the integration of:

- reaction kinetics;
- external mass transfer;
- internal diffusion;
- adsorption;
- reactant utilization;
- hydrodynamics;
- downstream separation;
- process-intensification penalties;
- validation and scale-up requirements.

The paper is an open scientific preprint. The repository is an E3 executable engineering-screening implementation.

Neither should be represented as a universally validated industrial design model.
